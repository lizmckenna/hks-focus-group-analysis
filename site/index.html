Context

As part of the Education Task Force's work on Professionalism as part of the HKS Vision and Strategy Process (VSP), SLATE convened five focus groups in February 2025 to collect structured student input on the teaching and learning environment. For each degree program (MPP1, MPP2, MCMPA, MPA/ID, and joint/dual degree), invitations were sent to a randomly selected sample of students. Participation was voluntary among those invited.

The conversations were guided by a broad definition of professionalism: the behaviors, expectations, and norms that foster a collaborative learning environment and prepare students for professional life. This lens applies in both directions, to student conduct and to faculty practice. Students were invited to reflect on what's working, what's not, and what they'd change.

A note on scope: Each program is represented by a single focus group. These are exploratory observations from specific conversations, not generalizable claims about program-wide opinion. See Methodology for full caveats.

What Students Value

Students across programs expressed genuine appreciation for aspects of the HKS teaching and learning environment. These observations are important context for the critiques that follow.

"It oftentimes exceeded my expectations in terms of rigor and also coherence between the different classes. I've been really impressed by the rigor, maybe too much at times where the pace has been difficult to manage." MPAID participant · On the MPAID core
"My experience with my professors here has been deeply inspiring. No matter whatever priorities they had, they treated me, their students, as their top priority." MCMPA participant · On faculty investment
"Because we have big cohort, the experiences are so diverse and so rich. We always learn from each other." MCMPA participant · On learning from peers

Key Findings

Ten themes emerged from the analysis, spanning student and faculty professionalism, classroom norms, assessment practices, and curriculum structure. A common thread: students want clarity, consistency, and mutual accountability.

Student Professionalism & Classroom Culture

Why this theme leads

Students often interpreted "professionalism" as career readiness rather than classroom conduct. This interpretive gap is important context for the findings that follow: many student responses are shaped by how they understood the framing question, not just by their experiences.

When asked about 'professionalism,' students interpret the term in two distinct ways: classroom conduct (behavior, punctuality, device use) and career preparation (whether the degree is making them job-ready). This mirrors the bidirectional definition that motivated these focus groups, where professionalism applies to both student responsibility and institutional responsibility. On conduct, MPAID students are most positive; joint-degree students with HBS experience are most critical. On career readiness, MCMPAs express the most concern. The key insight is that professionalism is not just about individual behavior. It is shaped by the incentives, norms, and enforcement structures the institution creates.

"About student conduct in class, I think that's very professional." MPAID participant · MPAID's positive baseline
"At the end of the day it's all about the professor's leadership that makes that class good." MPP2 participant · Faculty set the tone
"I think the professors are quite engaging. But oftentimes there's one or two people who take up the entirety of the class. In 75 percent of my classes, I've experienced this." MCMPA · Discussion domination problem
"Competitive. And it depends on professor." MPP1 · Two-word summary of MPP1 classroom culture
"I don't know if I can say I am a public administration professional." MIXED · MPA dual degree questioning career readiness

Core Curriculum: Structure, Flexibility, and Program Identity

Core curriculum is the theme that most clearly splits along degree lines. MPP students with a large required core want more flexibility and leveling. MPA dual-degree students with almost no core want more structure and coherence. MPAID students are broadly satisfied but want their core updated to reflect the evolving field. The shared request across all groups: let the core build toward something intentional, not just check boxes. Notably, MPP1s (currently navigating core requirements) express more frustration than MPP2s (who have more distance from the experience), suggesting that sentiment about the core may shift over the course of the degree.

"It's hard to have intentionality when you don't have any core. Most people actually don't know where they're going and they end up with a hodgepodge of classes that don't really align in any way." MIXED participant · MPA dual degree student on having too little structure
"We have too much core. I think that my degree feels very intentional. I know exactly what I'm doing." MIXED participant · MPP/HBS joint student, by contrast, has clear direction because of structured requirements
"When people say get rid of core classes, what they really mean is adjust for different learning levels. A degree has to have core requirements, but more optionality within core, adjusted to learning levels, is key." MPP1 · Not anti-core, but pro-leveling
"We need to stop thinking about topics and start thinking about functions. If we were to have an end-to-end workflow of something that a government does, what are the skills that I need? There needs to be a finance and budgeting class. There should be an operations class. There should be a leadership class." MIXED · Skills-based core redesign: build around professional functions, not academic departments
"The MPA/ID needs to be rethought. Some of these classes have not been restructured in 25 years." MIXED · Even the praised program needs updating

Feedback & Assessment: Timing, Quality, and Accountability

Assignment feedback emerged as one of the strongest themes across programs. Students describe a system with enormous variance: some professors provide detailed, timely feedback while others return grades weeks late with minimal comments, sometimes delegated entirely to CAs. The frustration is not just about speed. It is about the educational compact. Students who invest heavily in assignments feel shortchanged when feedback does not help them improve. The structural issue is clear: there are few school-wide standards for feedback timing or quality.

"I find it odd that there aren't more rules or structures around what professors can and can't do around assessment structure, around feedback expectations, timing. I feel like every class you go to, it's kind of a random gamble." MPP2 participant · Desire for structural consistency
"I had two classes last semester that I didn't get a single grade back the entire semester. I got A's in both the classes." MCMPA participant · Grades without feedback defeats the purpose
"I've had a very different experience. Not the timeline, but knowing that the instructor or professor will be reading every assignment and not a TA. I'm willing to wait that period so I get that full feedback. Very detailed feedback." MCMPA · Quality can compensate for speed
"We're expected to submit reviews of the professors before we even get our grades back at the end of the semester. I think that this is very typical." MCMPA · Student sees this timing problem as common, not exceptional
"One suggestion for the professor would be to just share a detailed rubric and a model answer. That's worked well in the courses that we've had them." MPAID · Concrete solution from students

Participation Grades vs. Class Size: A Structural Tension

This is one of the clearest structural findings: high participation grades (40 to 50%) in large classes (80 to 90 students) create perverse incentives. Students feel pressure to speak for the sake of being counted rather than to advance discussion. The result is lower-quality contributions, less airtime per student, and a dynamic where quantity crowds out depth. Students are not asking for smaller participation grades in small seminars. The problem is specifically the interaction between large enrollment and heavy participation weighting.

"Classes way too big. 80-90 people, and a 40 to 50 percent participation grade. People feel like they need to talk, but you can't have a conversation amongst a hundred people." MIXED participant · The core structural tension
"I think it's an intersection of class size and a huge participation grade. It really incentivizes people to speak for the sake of having their name noted rather than to advance the discussion." MPP1 participant · Perverse incentive
"It's also Teachly, from my understanding it tracks how many times you participate, which is a ridiculous measure of the quality of your contributions." MPP1 · Quantity over quality in tracking
"If you're going to have classes that large, you can't have a sizable participation grade. Or make the whole class for that day a discussion, like how it is at HBS." MIXED · Concrete alternative proposed
"We need to reduce class sizes. The size of the race and racism class makes me feel like the program doesn't actually care about race and racism." MPP1 · Class size as a signal about institutional priorities

Norm Enforcement & the Rules Gap

Students across programs identify a persistent gap between stated classroom norms and their enforcement. This tension sits at the intersection of student conduct and faculty accountability. Students recognize that punctuality, device use, and preparation are their responsibilities, but they also see enforcement as a faculty responsibility. When professors don't follow through on stated expectations, the norms lose meaning. The disconnect is sharpest in comparisons with HBS, where joint-degree students describe a culture of consistent consequences that HKS lacks. Students are not asking for draconian rules. They are asking for clarity about what is actually expected and consistency in how those expectations are upheld.

Key finding for deans

Students recognize that punctuality, device use, and preparation are their responsibilities, but they also see enforcement as a faculty responsibility.

"If someone walks in late, the teacher should say, this is your warning. The next class, get out. You're late. It's just establishing that if you cross this line, there's going to be a repercussion. The problem is that most lines at the Kennedy School, when you cross them, there's no repercussions." MIXED participant · Joint-degree student comparing to HBS
"Classes should start on time. Teachers should be ready five to ten minutes before. The norm should be that everyone is on time. The norm is that people aren't on their laptops in the back. I think less than 25% of the people on their computers in a Kennedy School class are actually taking notes." MIXED participant · On device culture
"I think the spoken rules always sound super reasonable and illegitimate. But behind those external rules, there are also implied rules that everyone understands what they are, but no one mentions them." MCMPA · On the gap between stated and actual norms
"I would definitely be keeping an eye out to see if the professor was treating everyone the same way. If anyone got a pass on this type of behavior, I'd see it much more negatively. Whereas if a professor is treating everyone the exact same way, and expectations were set clearly on the syllabus, I don't think I'd have a problem with it." MPP1 · Fairness and consistency matter more than strictness
"There is a certain merit to holding people to a certain academic standard. I genuinely feel that we should all be collectively holding ourselves to a better preparation for class. On one hand, I don't want other people to be late or underprepared. On the other hand, I don't want people to be intimidated." MPP1 · Tension between standards and psychological safety

Teaching Quality & Faculty Investment

Students distinguish clearly between the best and worst teaching experiences at HKS, and the gap is wide. The best professors are described as deeply prepared, pedagogically intentional, and invested in student learning. But quality is inconsistent, and students want to understand what incentives and structures exist to hold teaching quality accountable. The question of how HKS evaluates and develops its professors came up explicitly.

"My experience with my professors here has been deeply inspiring. No matter whatever priorities they had, they treated me, their students, as their top priority." MCMPA participant · Faculty investment at its best
"How does HKS assess professors? What are the incentives, and what are the performance mechanisms? Because I can see instances where there are amazing professors, but probably there's something to be learned when professors are not as strong." MCMPA participant · Asking about accountability structures
"From a professionalism standpoint, HKS is head and shoulders above the design school, where nobody pays attention in class whatsoever." MIXED · Favorable comparison from HGSD dual degree
"When I arrived at HKS and was told that we'd be doing case discussions, I had a different expectation than what I received in most of my classes." MPP2 · Gap between promise and delivery on case method
"They really reflected on what it means to learn. You can see it in the way that they teach." MCMPA · Pedagogical intentionality

Academic Rigor: Expectations Met and Unmet

Rigor is the theme that most sharply divides along program lines. MPAID students find it appropriately challenging and well-designed. MPP1s describe it as 'extremely underwhelming' with inconsistent standards. MCMPAs appreciate the democratic classroom culture but see discussions as sometimes performative rather than substantive. The common thread: rigor is not a simple dial. Students want the right level of challenge, clearly signaled expectations, and accountability for both preparation and engagement.

"I think it oftentimes exceeded my expectations in terms of rigor and also coherence between the different classes. I've been really impressed by the rigor, maybe too much at times where the pace has been difficult to manage." MPAID participant · MPAID positive experience
"When it comes to professionalism, I think HKS is very high, in a positive way. When it comes to rigor, I would say it's extremely underwhelming." MPP1 participant · Professionalism-rigor disconnect
"People should fail more classes. There's this very strong aversion in the culture to people being able to fail. Graduating students who put in very little effort damages the brand." MPP2 · Standards and consequences
"So the way of teaching is very different from what we have back home. It's more liberal and democratic in terms of how the class is conducted. And the openness of it, I think that's very positive." MCMPA · International student valuing HKS approach

Cold Calling

Cold calling generated genuinely split opinion, not along program lines but along pedagogical philosophy lines. Supporters see it as professionally relevant preparation for being put on the spot. Critics worry about its impact when overused or when it becomes the entire class dynamic rather than one tool among many. The nuance matters: students are not rejecting cold calling outright. They are asking for it to be deployed thoughtfully, with adequate warning about class format in syllabi.

"I dropped a class because of cold calling. The class that I really waited for, because the professor was doing cold calling in such a way that it was the whole essence of the class." MCMPA participant · When cold calling drives away engaged students
"I'm not against cold calling. I just think that sometimes if it's the whole essence of the class, maybe the syllabus should mention that." MCMPA participant · The ask is for transparency, not abolition
"Cold calling as a general thing is great. I think it can really enhance learning. But it's a spectrum. If you go to the other extreme where the focus is to just put people on the spot, that's different." MPP2 · Support for cold calling with limits
"I think cold calling, in my undergrad we actually don't have a lot of cold calling, because the class size is really large. So because at HKS the class size is smaller, I think it works." MPP2 · Class size determines effectiveness
"She was warm calling, and it was on reading. It would be in the beginning of class. And I thought it was effective because it held people accountable for doing the reading." MCMPA · Warm calling as a middle ground

Faculty Accessibility & Office Hours

Office hours surfaced as a concrete access point where structural constraints become visible. Large class sizes mean limited slots. Some professors open office hours to any HKS student, diluting access for enrolled students. Scheduling friction (inconsistent platforms, limited windows) compounds the problem. Students frame this as a professional relationship issue: they want more than transactional Q&A sessions.

"Accessibility towards students should be a core performance metric for teachers. It's unacceptable that there are some professors who are saying, I only have one office hour a week for a class of 60 students." MCMPA participant · Structural mismatch between class size and access
"The office hours are open for everyone, even students who don't take the professor's class. So the student can probably find a time, but that means fewer slots for enrolled students." MPAID participant · Open access dilutes availability
"Consistent formatting for office hours would help. I have had a professor who used Calendly, and I didn't realize they updated every two weeks." MPP2 · Small friction points add up
"It's all about the expectations. A lot of students expect coaching feedback, but it's fair to say office hours is a better venue for coaching than writing up a paragraph on an assignment." MPP2 · Realistic about what feedback channels can deliver

Reading Volume & Workload Sustainability

Students across programs (especially MCMPAs and MPP1s) describe reading volumes that are unrealistic and, crucially, often disconnected from what happens in class. The frustration is not with hard work. It is with work that feels pointless when professors do not draw on assigned readings during sessions. MPAID students, by contrast, praised their program office's deliberate workload management as a model.

"Reduce the volume of work. Not necessarily assignments, but the readings. I feel like I'm getting such a surface-level understanding of so much, and I'm not getting a deep understanding of anything." MPP1 participant · Breadth over depth frustration
"There are some professors where it's 400 pages of readings for one class, and you're like, okay, well, what is the point of this?" MCMPA participant · Volume without purpose
"It would be very helpful if before we are starting a course, we can get our course packets. That happens in HLS. That happens in HBS." MCMPA · Pre-enrollment materials as standard at peer schools
"There should be a meeting for arranging volume of reading among professors. Last semester, one of my classes gave me 200 pages per week." MPP1 · No cross-course coordination
"The program office manages the workload calendar. They really put a lot of thought into it and make sure that we don't have too many assignments due in one week. That really helps a lot in the learning culture overall." MPAID · MPAID's workload management as a model
